Can a spinor field on a Poisson sprinkling have a Lorentz-covariant kinetic term built from the causal order alone? There are two answers, each with conditions. If the field is a Weyl spinor attached to each element, with the same spinor space, and hence the same Lorentz action, at every element, and if the quadratic form is covariant, then in four dimensions the only order-natural kernels are Majorana mass terms and no kinetic term exists (Theorem 1). The obstruction is that a first-order operator needs a vector-valued datum on each pair of elements, and the order supplies none. If instead the field is a Kähler–Dirac field, a cochain on the order complex, the pointwise obstruction disappears and another takes its place. The index of any operator that is odd under the parity of the cochain degree equals the Euler characteristic of the complex; on a Poisson sprinkling this is minus a number that grows faster than the number of elements, so such an operator has at least that many exact zero modes against the $$2^{d/2}$$ species expected in $$d$$ dimensions (Theorem 5 and Proposition 7). In two dimensions, on the complex generated by links and pairs with one interior element, no Hodge-type Wilson term and no ultralocal degree mass leaves light modes (section 4). In 1+1 dimensions a one-handed fermion exists on the order chain once a binary choice of null direction is made, but its vacuum contains the mirror fermion (section 5).

Matter on causal sets has been developed mostly for scalar fields: Johnston obtained retarded propagators from a sum over chains [[Johnston 2008]](#ref-johnston) [[Johnston thesis]](#ref-jthesis), and these were examined in curved backgrounds by Nomaan X, Dowker and Surya [[X–Dowker–Surya]](#ref-ads); a review of the programme is [[Surya]](#ref-surya). For spinors, Sverdlov builds vierbeins from holonomies and then spinor fields on them [[Sverdlov]](#ref-sverdlov), which supplies frame data per element beyond the order. Dowker and Sorkin proved that a Poisson sprinkling of Minkowski space carries no distinguished spatial or temporal orientation, no preferred lattice of timelike directions, and no distinguished lattice of points [[Dowker–Sorkin]](#ref-ds). Kähler–Dirac fermions, whose degrees of freedom are cochains and whose coboundary is combinatorial, were put on triangulations by Catterall and collaborators, with the index given by the Euler characteristic [[Catterall–Laiho–Unmuth-Yockey]](#ref-clu) [[Butt et al.]](#ref-butt) and a reduced version with mirror fermions [[Catterall]](#ref-catterall); the Lorentzian version needs an operator that is incompatible with Lorentz transformations [[Catterall–Hubisz]](#ref-ch). The relation between the Euler characteristic of a poset's order complex and its Möbius function is due to P. Hall (see [[Wachs]](#ref-wachs)). This page adds: Theorem 1 with its hypotheses written out; the computation of the Euler characteristic of the span-restricted order complex of a sprinkling, with the exact expected number of links in two dimensions; the statements on Wilson terms in two dimensions and the identity behind them; and the mirror theorem for the order chain in 1+1 dimensions.

Frames cannot be supplied by a sprinkling at random: [Where a preferred frame leaks into low-energy physics](/quantum-gravity/lorentz-violation-leakage/) proves that there is no Lorentz-invariant probability measure on frames, and [Lorentz-invariant quantum sprinklings](/quantum-gravity/quantum-sprinklings/) and [Lorentz-invariant point sets](/quantum-gravity/invariant-point-sets/) describe the sprinklings themselves. Operators that are built without any frame and what they can and cannot carry as chirality are treated in [Frame-free operators carry no chirality index](/quantum-gravity/frame-free-chirality/); frames used inside a Euclidean regulator are treated in [Frames, Wilson terms and reflection positivity for fermions on random points](/quantum-gravity/frames-and-reflection-positivity/).

## 1. Causal sets, sprinklings and "order-only"

A causal set is a locally finite set $$P$$ with a strict partial order $$\prec$$. For $$x\prec y$$ the interval is $$I(x,y)=\{z:x\prec z\prec y\}$$, and $$x\prec y$$ is a link if $$I(x,y)=\varnothing$$. A sprinkling of density $$\rho$$ into Minkowski space $$\mathbb M^{d}$$ is a Poisson point process with intensity $$\rho$$, ordered by $$x\prec y$$ when $$y$$ lies in the causal future of $$x$$. For finite computations we sprinkle into a causal diamond. In two dimensions, with null coordinates $$(u,v)$$ scaled so that the diamond is the unit square, $$x\prec y$$ means $$u_x<u_y$$ and $$v_x<v_y$$.

Every orthochronous Poincaré transformation $$g$$ maps $$\mathbb M^{d}$$ to itself preserving the causal order, so it maps a point set $$P$$ to a point set $$gP$$ with an isomorphic causal order, the isomorphism being $$x\mapsto gx$$. A construction that uses only the order is therefore *natural*: what it assigns to a pair $$(x,y)$$ in $$P$$ equals what it assigns to $$(gx,gy)$$ in $$gP$$. This is the sense in which a causal-set construction is Lorentz invariant, and it is what makes the question below sharp: if the construction also has to be covariant on spinors, naturality and covariance together constrain each value.

## 2. Weyl spinors and the Schur argument

**Weyl spinors.** A left-handed Weyl spinor in four dimensions is a pair $$\psi\in\mathbb C^{2}$$ on which $$S\in SL(2,\mathbb C)$$ acts as $$\psi\mapsto S\psi$$. The connection with Lorentz transformations is this. Identify a vector $$x=(x^{0},\vec x)$$ with the Hermitian matrix $$X=x^{0}\mathbb 1+\vec x\cdot\vec\sigma$$, so that $$\det X=(x^{0})^{2}-\lvert\vec x\rvert^{2}$$. The map $$X\mapsto SXS^{\dagger}$$ preserves Hermiticity and the determinant, hence is a Lorentz transformation $$\Lambda(S)$$, and $$S\mapsto\Lambda(S)$$ is a two-to-one cover of the proper orthochronous group $$SO^{+}(1,3)$$ with kernel $$\pm\mathbb 1$$. A rotation by $$2\pi$$ corresponds to $$S=-\mathbb 1$$, so spinors change sign. The matrix $$\psi\psi^{\dagger}$$ transforms as $$X$$ does, which is why the combinations $$\psi^{\dagger}\bar\sigma^{\mu}\psi$$, with $$\bar\sigma^{\mu}=(\mathbb 1,-\vec\sigma)$$, are the components of a vector, and why the kinetic term $$\psi^{\dagger}\bar\sigma^{\mu}\partial_\mu\psi$$ requires a vector-valued object: the four Hermitian matrices $$\bar\sigma^{\mu}$$ form a basis of the space $$\mathrm{Herm}(2)\cong\mathbb R^{1,3}$$ of Hermitian $$2\times2$$ matrices. A mass term is a quadratic form in $$\psi$$ without a derivative. For anticommuting components the only candidate is $$\psi^{T}\epsilon\psi$$ with $$\epsilon$$ the antisymmetric matrix, a Majorana mass. In two dimensions a chiral Weyl spinor has one component, and a boost with rapidity $$\eta$$ acts as multiplication by $$e^{\eta/2}$$. In three dimensions the spinor group is $$SL(2,\mathbb R)$$ acting on $$\mathbb C^{2}$$.

**What a construction is.** Fix $$d\in\{2,3,4\}$$ and let $$V$$ be the spinor space, with $$S(g)$$ the matrix by which $$g$$ in the double cover of $$SO^{+}(1,d-1)$$ acts. A *construction* assigns to every locally finite point set $$P\subset\mathbb M^{d}$$ and every ordered pair $$(x,y)$$ of its elements a matrix $$\Phi(P)(x,y)$$ in $$\mathrm{Herm}(V)$$, so that the quadratic form is $$\sum_{x,y}\psi_x^{\dagger}\Phi(P)(x,y)\psi_y$$ with $$\psi_x\in V$$. A bilinear construction assigns instead a matrix defining $$\sum\psi_x^{T}\Phi(P)(x,y)\psi_y$$. We use three hypotheses.

(H1) There is one copy of $$V$$ at every element, and a Lorentz transformation $$g$$ acts on all copies by the same matrix $$S(g)$$. This is a global trivialisation of the spinor bundle, that is a global frame.

(H2) $$\Phi$$ depends on the order alone: $$\Phi(gP)(gx,gy)=\Phi(P)(x,y)$$ for every orthochronous proper Lorentz transformation $$g$$ and, if a region frame is added, for the stabiliser of that frame.

(H3) The quadratic form is covariant: replacing $$P$$ by $$gP$$ and $$\psi_x$$ by $$S(g)\psi_x$$ at the element $$gx$$ leaves its value unchanged, which means $$S(g)^{\dagger}\Phi(gP)(gx,gy)S(g)=\Phi(P)(x,y)$$.

Combining (H2) and (H3) gives, for every $$g$$ in the group $$H$$ under consideration, every $$P$$, $$x$$ and $$y$$,

$$
S(g)^{\dagger}\,\Phi(P)(x,y)\,S(g)=\Phi(P)(x,y), \tag{1}
$$

and the same with transposes for bilinear constructions. So each value of the kernel is an $$H$$-invariant form on a single copy of $$V$$, and the problem is reduced to representation theory. The groups $$H$$ are the proper orthochronous Lorentz group $$L$$; the stabiliser of a causal diamond (a pair of related points), which is trivial in $$d=2$$, $$SO(2)$$ in $$d=3$$ and $$SO(3)$$, acting through $$SU(2)$$, in $$d=4$$; and the trivial group.

<div class="theorem" markdown="1">
**Theorem 1** (order-natural covariant spinor kernels). Under (H1) to (H3) every value $$\Phi(P)(x,y)$$ is a form on $$V$$ invariant under $$H$$. The real dimensions of the spaces of invariant Hermitian forms and of invariant bilinear forms are

| $$d$$ | $$H$$ | Hermitian forms | bilinear forms |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | $$L$$ | 0 | 2 |
| 4 | $$SU(2)$$ (diamond) | 1 | 2 |
| 4 | trivial | 4 | 8 |
| 3 | $$L$$ | 1 | 2 |
| 3 | $$SO(2)$$ (diamond) | 2 | 4 |
| 2 | $$L$$ | 0 | 0 |
| 2 | trivial (diamond) | 1 | 2 |

In particular, in four dimensions with $$H=L$$ every order-natural covariant sesquilinear kernel vanishes, and the only order-natural covariant bilinear kernels are multiples of $$\epsilon$$ (Majorana masses). With a diamond added, a sesquilinear kernel is a scalar function times $$\mathbb 1$$ and distinguishes no helicity.
</div>

<div class="proof" markdown="1">
*Proof.* The idea is that an invariant form is an intertwiner, and the Weyl representation has no invariant Hermitian pairing with itself because its conjugate is a different representation. For $$d=4$$ we give the computation for each row.

*Sesquilinear forms, $$H=L$$.* A Hermitian $$k$$ is a vector in $$\mathrm{Herm}(2)\cong\mathbb R^{1,3}$$, and $$k\mapsto S^{\dagger}kS$$ is the Lorentz transformation $$\Lambda(S^{\dagger})$$. As $$S$$ runs over $$SL(2,\mathbb C)$$ so does $$S^{\dagger}$$, so an invariant $$k$$ is a vector fixed by all of $$SO^{+}(1,3)$$. Rotations fix only vectors along the time axis, and a boost along $$x^1$$ moves $$(t,0,0,0)$$ to $$(t\cosh\eta,t\sinh\eta,0,0)$$. So $$k=0$$.

*Bilinear forms, $$H=L$$.* Take $$S=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda,\lambda^{-1})$$ with $$\lambda\neq\pm1$$ in $$S^{T}kS=k$$: this gives $$k_{11}\lambda^{2}=k_{11}$$ and $$k_{22}\lambda^{-2}=k_{22}$$, so $$k_{11}=k_{22}=0$$. With $$k=\begin{pmatrix}0&b\\c&0\end{pmatrix}$$ and $$S=\begin{pmatrix}1&a\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$$ one finds the $$(2,2)$$ entry of $$S^{T}kS$$ equal to $$ab+ca$$, which must vanish, so $$c=-b$$ and $$k=b\epsilon$$. Conversely $$S^{T}\epsilon S=\epsilon$$ for $$\det S=1$$. One complex parameter is two real.

*$$H=SU(2)$$.* Here $$\mathrm{Herm}(2)=\mathbb R\mathbb 1\oplus\mathbb R^{3}$$ and $$S^{\dagger}kS=S^{-1}kS$$ rotates the Pauli part, so the invariants are the multiples of $$\mathbb 1$$. For bilinear forms $$S^{T}=\epsilon S^{-1}\epsilon^{-1}$$ for $$S\in SU(2)$$, so the condition says that $$\epsilon^{-1}k$$ commutes with $$SU(2)$$, hence is a scalar, and $$k\propto\epsilon$$.

*Trivial group.* All Hermitian $$2\times2$$ matrices (4 real parameters) and all complex $$2\times2$$ matrices (8 real parameters).

For $$d=2$$ the space $$V=\mathbb C$$ carries the character $$S=e^{\eta/2}$$, and invariance requires $$e^{\eta}k=k$$ for Hermitian forms and $$e^{\eta}k=k$$ for bilinear forms, so $$k=0$$ when $$H$$ contains boosts, and any value otherwise. The rows for $$d=3$$ are the same computation with $$SL(2,\mathbb R)$$ and its rotation subgroup. The dimensions in the table were also found by solving $$S^{\dagger}kS=k$$ (or $$S^{T}kS=k$$) numerically for random group elements, with the same results in all fourteen entries. $$\square$$
</div>

The computation is routine. The content of the theorem is the list of hypotheses: (H1) in particular is what fails if the spinor space at each element is allowed to depend on the element (local frames), and it is the same requirement that, in the continuum, makes a spinor kinetic term depend on a choice of tetrad in addition to the metric.

<div class="corollary" markdown="1">
**Corollary 2** (no order-natural Weyl operator in four dimensions). Let $$K$$ be an operator on smooth fields given by $$(K\psi)_x=\sum_y\Phi(P)(x,y)\psi_y$$ with $$\Phi$$ satisfying (H1) to (H3) and $$H=L$$. Then $$\Phi=0$$ and $$K=0$$. With a diamond added, $$K$$ acts on each component of $$\psi$$ by the same scalar kernel, and no term in the expansion of $$K\psi$$ in derivatives of $$\psi$$ depends on its spin.
</div>

*Proof.* The first statement is Theorem 1. For the second, $$\Phi(P)(x,y)=\varphi(P)(x,y)\mathbb 1$$, so $$K$$ commutes with the rotation of the components. A Weyl operator $$\vec\sigma\cdot\vec\nabla$$ does not. $$\square$$

*Example* (square roots). A familiar way to construct a Dirac-type propagator from an order-natural matrix $$\Phi$$ is to look for a square root. For any matrix $$\Phi$$, with $$\sigma_\pm$$ the raising and lowering matrices of a two-component space and $$\lambda\neq0$$, the matrix $$R_\lambda=-\rho\lambda\,\Phi\otimes\sigma_{+}+\lambda^{-1}\mathbb 1\otimes\sigma_{-}$$ satisfies $$R_\lambda^{2}=-\rho\,\Phi\otimes\mathbb 1$$, because $$\sigma_\pm^{2}=0$$ and $$\sigma_+\sigma_-+\sigma_-\sigma_+=\mathbb 1$$. The roots form a one-parameter family. In 1+1 dimensions a boost acts on the two components as $$\mathrm{diag}(e^{\eta/2},e^{-\eta/2})$$ and maps $$R_\lambda$$ to $$R_{\lambda e^{\eta}}$$. So the ambiguity of the root is a boost orbit, and fixing a root fixes a frame, consistent with Theorem 1.

**Frames that are supplied.** The obstruction is removed by data not contained in the order: a frame. We record two statements about such data.

<div class="proposition" markdown="1">
**Proposition 3** (no random global frame). Let $$\mathcal P$$ be a Poisson sprinkling of $$\mathbb M^{4}$$ and let $$F$$ be a random element of $$SL(2,\mathbb C)$$ (a global frame) jointly distributed with $$\mathcal P$$ so that the pair $$(\mathcal P,F)$$ has a law invariant under $$g:(P,F)\mapsto(gP,S(g)F)$$. There is no such law.
</div>

<div class="proof" markdown="1">
*Proof.* The map $$F\mapsto FF^{\dagger}$$ takes values in the positive Hermitian matrices of determinant 1, which is the hyperboloid $$H^{3}$$ of future unit timelike vectors, and is equivariant: $$(S F)(SF)^{\dagger}=S(FF^{\dagger})S^{\dagger}=\Lambda(S)\cdot(FF^{\dagger})$$. The law of $$FF^{\dagger}$$ would be a Lorentz-invariant probability measure on $$H^{3}$$, which does not exist by Proposition 6 of [Where a preferred frame leaks into low-energy physics](/quantum-gravity/lorentz-violation-leakage/). $$\square$$
</div>

A frame cannot therefore be a random variable attached to the sprinkling with an invariant joint law. It can be a fixed choice, which breaks Lorentz invariance, or it can be gauge data attached to each element, in which case the kernel depends on the frames at its two ends and (H1) fails. The other statement concerns link operators once a frame is given.

<div class="proposition" markdown="1">
**Proposition 4** (link operators with non-negative weights). Let $$u$$ be a unit timelike frame, let $$\Delta_n\in\mathbb M^{4}$$ be a finite set of future-causal vectors (displacements between linked elements) and let $$w_n\ge0$$, not all zero. Define the symbol $$F(p)=-2\sum_nw_n\,\bar\sigma\!\cdot\!\Delta_n\,\sin(p\!\cdot\!\Delta_n)$$. Then $$M_{\mu\nu}=\sum_nw_n\Delta_{n\mu}\Delta_{n\nu}$$ is a non-zero positive semidefinite matrix, hence is not proportional to the Minkowski metric, and $$F(p)=-2\bar\sigma^{\mu}M_{\mu\nu}p^{\nu}+O(p^{3})$$. If the weights are rotation invariant about $$u$$, so that $$M=\mathrm{diag}(a,b,b,b)$$ in the frame of $$u$$, the infrared symbol has the relative sign of the time and space terms opposite to that of a covariant Weyl symbol $$\bar\sigma^{\mu}\eta_{\mu\nu}p^{\nu}$$, that is the opposite helicity, and its speed is $$b/a\le\tfrac13$$, with equality only if every $$\Delta_n$$ is null.
</div>

<div class="proof" markdown="1">
*Proof.* $$M$$ is a sum of positive semidefinite rank-one matrices, and non-zero because $$\Delta_n\neq0$$ for a causal vector. A multiple $$\lambda\eta$$ with $$\lambda\neq0$$ is indefinite, and $$\lambda=0$$ would make $$M=0$$. Expanding the sine gives the leading term. In signature $$(-+++)$$ with $$\bar\sigma^{\mu}=(\mathbb 1,-\vec\sigma)$$, $$\bar\sigma^{\mu}\eta_{\mu\nu}p^{\nu}=-(p^{0}+\vec\sigma\!\cdot\!\vec p)$$, whereas $$\bar\sigma^{\mu}M_{\mu\nu}p^{\nu}=ap^{0}-b\,\vec\sigma\!\cdot\!\vec p$$: the relative sign of the two terms is reversed. The speed is $$b/a$$. Since each $$\Delta_n$$ is causal, $$(\Delta^{0}_n)^{2}\ge\lvert\vec\Delta_n\rvert^{2}$$, so $$a=\sum w(\Delta^{0})^{2}\ge\sum w\lvert\vec\Delta\rvert^{2}=3b$$. $$\square$$
</div>

A covariant infrared limit therefore needs an indefinite coefficient matrix, which in the setting of Proposition 3 means signed weights or a frame-dependent twist. The proposition makes no statement about the absence of doublers: it concerns the infrared symbol only, and the location of other zeros of the symbol depends on the weights.

## 3. Kähler–Dirac fields on the order complex

**Forms as fermions.** In flat space the exterior derivative $$d$$ and its adjoint $$\delta$$ act on the space $$\Omega=\bigoplus_{k=0}^{d}\Omega^{k}$$ of differential forms, and $$K=d+\delta$$ satisfies $$K^{2}=d\delta+\delta d=$$ the Hodge Laplacian. The exterior and interior products with a vector define a Clifford action on $$\Omega$$, so $$\Omega\cong\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb R^{d})$$ is a sum of $$2^{d/2}$$ copies of the Dirac spinor representation, the "tastes", and $$K$$ is the Dirac operator on this sum (Kähler). The parity $$\epsilon=(-1)^{k}$$ of the degree anticommutes with $$K$$. On a lattice or a triangulation the same construction is made with cochains: $$C^{k}$$ is the space of functions on $$k$$-simplices and $$d$$ the coboundary, and the index of $$K$$ is related to the Euler characteristic of the complex [[Catterall–Laiho–Unmuth-Yockey]](#ref-clu) [[Butt et al.]](#ref-butt). No frame is needed to define $$d$$, and this is why Kähler–Dirac fields can escape Theorem 1: their degrees of freedom sit on tuples of elements and not in a fibre of a spinor bundle.

**The complex.** For a causal set $$P$$ the simplices are chains $$x_0\prec x_1\prec\dots\prec x_k$$, with the coboundary

$$
(d\omega)(x_0,\dots,x_{k+1})=\sum_{i=0}^{k+1}(-1)^{i}\,\omega(x_0,\dots,\widehat{x_i},\dots,x_{k+1}). \tag{2}
$$

The order complex of all chains is not local: a chain may contain elements far apart. We restrict to chains whose first and last elements have at most $$n_0$$ elements between them, and call the resulting simplicial complex $$\Delta_{n_0}$$. A sub-chain of such a chain has a smaller span, so $$\Delta_{n_0}$$ is closed under faces. A chain with $$k+1$$ elements has $$k-1$$ elements inside its span, so $$\dim\Delta_{n_0}\le n_0+1$$ and the choice $$n_0=d-1$$ gives a complex of dimension at most $$d$$. For $$d=2$$ the complex $$\Delta_1$$ has as vertices the elements, as edges the links and the pairs $$x\prec z$$ with exactly one element $$y$$ in between, and as triangles the chains $$x\prec y\prec z$$ with $$I(x,z)=\{y\}$$.

The cochain spaces are made into inner product spaces by a nondegenerate bilinear form $$M=\bigoplus_kM_k$$ on each degree separately. The default is the counting form $$M_k=\mathbb 1$$, for which the adjoint of $$d$$ is the transpose $$d^{T}$$; another is the Krein form $$M_k=(-1)^{k}\mathbb 1$$, indefinite, for which the adjoint is $$-d^{T}$$. We write $$d^{*}$$ for the adjoint and $$K=d+d^{*}$$.

<div class="theorem" markdown="1">
**Theorem 5** (index of parity-odd operators). Let $$\Delta$$ be a finite simplicial complex with cochain spaces $$C^{k}$$, let $$M$$ be a nondegenerate degree-diagonal bilinear form, and let $$K$$ be a linear operator on $$C=\bigoplus_kC^{k}$$ that maps even to odd degrees and odd to even degrees and is self-adjoint for $$M$$. Then

$$
\dim\ker K|_{\mathrm{even}}-\dim\ker K|_{\mathrm{odd}}=\chi(\Delta)=\sum_k(-1)^{k}f_k, \tag{3}
$$

where $$f_k$$ is the number of $$k$$-simplices. Hence $$\dim\ker K\ge\lvert\chi(\Delta)\rvert$$. If $$M$$ is positive definite and $$K=d+d^{*}$$, then $$\dim\ker K=\sum_kb_k$$, the sum of the Betti numbers.
</div>

<div class="proof" markdown="1">
*Proof.* The point is that rank–nullity applied to the two halves of $$K$$ gives the index, and self-adjointness makes the two ranks equal. Write $$K_+=K|_{\mathrm{even}}:C^{e}\to C^{o}$$ and $$K_-=K|_{\mathrm{odd}}$$. For $$x$$ even and $$y$$ odd, self-adjointness gives $$\langle K_+x,y\rangle_M=\langle x,K_-y\rangle_M$$. Since $$M$$ is nondegenerate on $$C^{e}$$, $$y\perp\mathrm{ran}\,K_+$$ if and only if $$K_-y=0$$: $$\mathrm{ran}(K_+)^{\perp}=\ker K_-$$. For a subspace $$W$$ of a space with a nondegenerate form $$\dim W^{\perp}=\dim C^{o}-\dim W$$, so $$\dim\ker K_-=\dim C^{o}-\mathrm{rk}K_+$$, while $$\dim\ker K_+=\dim C^{e}-\mathrm{rk}K_+$$. Subtracting gives $$\dim C^{e}-\dim C^{o}=\sum(-1)^{k}f_k$$, and $$\ker K=\ker K_+\oplus\ker K_-$$ gives the bound. For positive $$M$$, $$\langle d\omega,d^{*}\eta\rangle=\langle dd\omega,\eta\rangle=0$$, so $$\lVert K\omega\rVert^{2}=\lVert d\omega\rVert^{2}+\lVert d^{*}\omega\rVert^{2}$$, $$\ker K=\ker d\cap\ker d^{*}$$, and Hodge theory identifies this with the cohomology. $$\square$$
</div>

The statement is the discrete McKean–Singer index relation and is routine; the content for causal sets is in the size of $$\chi$$. For a poset the Euler characteristic of the complexes $$\Delta_{n_0}$$ is computed from the Möbius function $$\mu$$, defined by $$\mu(x,x)=1$$ and $$\sum_{x\preceq z\preceq y}\mu(x,z)=0$$ for $$x\prec y$$. Hall's theorem says $$\mu(x,y)=\sum_{k\ge1}(-1)^{k}c_k(x,y)$$, where $$c_k$$ is the number of chains $$x=x_0\prec\dots\prec x_k=y$$ [[Wachs]](#ref-wachs).

<div class="corollary" markdown="1">
**Corollary 6** (Euler characteristic of the span-restricted complex). With $$N=\lvert P\rvert$$,

$$
\chi(\Delta_{n_0})=N+\sum_{\substack{x\prec y\\ \lvert I(x,y)\rvert\le n_0}}\mu(x,y), \tag{4}
$$

and $$\chi(\Delta_1)=N-L$$ where $$L$$ is the number of links.
</div>

<div class="proof" markdown="1">
*Proof.* The $$N$$ one-element chains contribute $$+1$$ each. Group the other simplices by their first and last elements $$x\prec y$$. If $$\lvert I(x,y)\rvert\le n_0$$ then every chain from $$x$$ to $$y$$ is a simplex of $$\Delta_{n_0}$$, since all its elements lie in $$I(x,y)$$, and the signed count is $$\mu(x,y)$$ by Hall's theorem. A link has the single chain $$x\prec y$$, so $$\mu=-1$$; a pair with one interior element $$z$$ has the chains $$x\prec y$$ and $$x\prec z\prec y$$, so $$\mu=-1+1=0$$. This gives (4) for $$n_0=1$$. The same follows directly from the face counts of $$\Delta_1$$: the triangles are in bijection with the one-interior pairs, because a triangle $$x\prec y\prec z$$ has $$I(x,z)=\{y\}$$, so $$f_0-f_1+f_2=N-(L+T)+T=N-L$$. $$\square$$
</div>

*Example.* Take the poset with four elements $$x\prec a,b\prec y$$, $$a$$ and $$b$$ unrelated. It has four links, and $$I(x,y)=\{a,b\}$$ has two elements. Then $$\chi(\Delta_1)=4-4=0$$. For $$n_0=2$$ the pair $$(x,y)$$ is admitted with $$\mu(x,y)=-(1-1-1)=1$$, and $$\chi(\Delta_2)=4-4+1=1$$, in agreement with the face count $$4-5+2=1$$ and with the fact that this complex is a cone, hence contractible.

**How large is $$\chi$$ on a sprinkling?** A link is a pair with an empty interval, and in a Lorentz-invariant sprinkling an element has unboundedly many links as the density grows [[Bombelli–Henson–Sorkin]](#ref-bhs). In two dimensions the expected number of links can be computed exactly.

<div class="proposition" markdown="1">
**Proposition 7** (expected links in two dimensions). Let $$N$$ points be independent and uniform in the unit square, ordered by the product order (a sprinkling of the causal diamond in null coordinates, with the number of points fixed). Then

$$
\mathbb E[L]=N\,H_{N-1}+H_N-2N+1,\qquad H_n=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac1k, \tag{5}
$$

and consequently $$\mathbb E[\chi(\Delta_1)]=-N\bigl(\ln N+\gamma-3\bigr)+O(\ln N)$$, where $$\gamma$$ is Euler's constant.
</div>

<div class="proof" markdown="1">
*Proof.* Count ordered pairs $$(i,j)$$. For $$i\neq j$$ the differences $$s=u_j-u_i$$ and $$t=v_j-v_i$$ are both positive with joint density $$(1-s)(1-t)$$ on the unit square, and given them $$(i,j)$$ is a link exactly when none of the other $$N-2$$ points falls in the rectangle of area $$st$$ between them, which has probability $$(1-st)^{N-2}$$. With $$m=N-2$$,

$$
\mathbb E[L]=N(N-1)\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^1(1-s)(1-t)(1-st)^{m}\,ds\,dt .
$$

Expand $$(1-s)(1-t)=1-s-t+st$$. The product $$p=st$$ of two uniform variables has density $$-\ln p$$, so $$\iint(1-st)^{m}=\int_0^1(-\ln p)(1-p)^{m}dp=H_{m+1}/(m+1)$$. Next $$\iint s(1-st)^{m}=\int_0^1\frac{1-(1-s)^{m+1}}{m+1}\,ds=\frac1{m+2}$$, and the same for $$t$$. Finally $$\iint st(1-st)^{m}=\int_0^1(-\ln p)\,p(1-p)^{m}dp=\frac{H_{m+2}-1}{(m+1)(m+2)}$$. Adding the four terms and multiplying by $$N(N-1)$$ gives (5). The asymptotics follow from $$H_{N-1}=\ln N+\gamma+O(1/N)$$ and Corollary 6. $$\square$$
</div>

The routine part is the integral; the step with content is that the pairs of elements with an empty rectangle between them are not rare, which is the discreteness-without-symmetry-breaking phenomenon in two dimensions. Equation (5) was checked at $$N=2$$ (value $$1/2$$) and $$N=3$$ (value $$4/3$$) by hand and against Monte Carlo sampling below. Table 1 gives sample means of $$-\chi/N$$ for $$\Delta_{d-1}$$ together with the exact expectation in two dimensions. In four dimensions $$\chi(\Delta_3)$$ was computed from (4), with $$\mu$$ evaluated by the recursion $$\mu(x,y)=-1-\sum_{z\in I(x,y)}\mu(x,z)$$ for all pairs with at most three interior elements, on points uniform in a causal diamond.

<div class="corollary" markdown="1">
**Corollary 8** (zero modes of parity-odd operators). For every nondegenerate degree-diagonal $$M$$ and every $$M$$-self-adjoint operator $$K$$ that exchanges even and odd degrees on $$\Delta_{d-1}$$ of a sprinkling, in particular for $$d+d^{*}$$ with the counting or the Krein form, $$\dim\ker K\ge\lvert\chi(\Delta_{d-1})\rvert$$. In two dimensions $$\mathbb E\lvert\chi\rvert=N(\ln N+\gamma-3)+O(\ln N)$$. In the sample of Table 1 at $$N=4000$$ this is at least about $$23\,600$$ exact zero modes in two dimensions, and at $$N=4000$$ in four dimensions, with $$\lvert\chi\rvert/N=17.1$$, at least about $$68\,000$$, against $$2^{d/2}=2$$ and $$4$$ tastes.
</div>

The comparison with $$2^{d/2}$$ is a comparison with the zero modes of a Dirac operator on a region of fixed topology, whose number is bounded independently of the density, and so it concerns the physical interpretation and not the theorem. The lower bound itself is Theorem 5; the growth of $$\chi$$ is proved in expectation for $$d=2$$ (Proposition 7) and measured for $$d=4$$.

## 4. Wilson-type terms on the two-dimensional complex

The index relation (3) holds for operators that anticommute with $$\epsilon$$. A Wilson term, such as $$r\,K^{2}$$, commutes with $$\epsilon$$, and the zero modes in (3) can then be lifted. We ask whether any such term leaves light modes. Take $$d=2$$, the complex $$\Delta_1$$ with cochain spaces $$C^{0},C^{1},C^{2}$$, $$d_0:C^{0}\to C^{1}$$, $$d_1:C^{1}\to C^{2}$$, the counting form, $$K=d+d^{T}$$ and Hodge Laplacians $$L_0=d_0^{T}d_0$$, $$L_2=d_1d_1^{T}$$. Write $$b_k$$ for the Betti numbers of $$\Delta_1$$ and $$\Pi_1$$ for the projector on $$C^{1}$$.

<div class="proposition" markdown="1">
**Proposition 9** (Hodge-type terms do not lift). Let $$W$$ be any element of the non-unital algebra generated by $$d$$ and $$d^{T}$$ (it contains $$rK^{2}$$) and $$\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb C$$. Then $$\ker(\alpha K+\beta W)\supseteq\ker K$$, and on $$\Delta_1$$ $$\dim\ker(\alpha K+\beta W)\ge\sum_kb_k\ge L-N+1$$.
</div>

<div class="proof" markdown="1">
*Proof.* By Theorem 5, $$h\in\ker K$$ implies $$dh=0$$ and $$d^{T}h=0$$. Every word in $$d$$ and $$d^{T}$$ acts first with one of them, hence annihilates $$h$$. For the count, $$b_0-b_1+b_2=\chi=N-L$$ by Corollary 6, $$b_2=\dim\ker L_2=0$$ by Proposition 10 below, and $$b_0\ge1$$, so $$b_1=b_0+L-N\ge L-N+1$$. $$\square$$
</div>

The proof uses that the Wilson term is built from the same operators as $$K$$. The next statement is about the structure of $$\Delta_1$$ and holds for every finite poset.

<div class="proposition" markdown="1">
**Proposition 10** (private faces). For every finite poset, $$L_2=d_1d_1^{T}$$ on $$\Delta_1$$ satisfies $$L_2\ge\mathbb 1$$. Hence $$b_2=0$$, and the nonzero spectrum of $$K^{2}$$ is the union of the nonzero spectrum of $$L_0$$ and the spectrum of $$L_2$$, the latter lying in $$[1,\infty)$$.
</div>

<div class="proof" markdown="1">
*Proof.* The idea is that each triangle has an edge that belongs to no other triangle, so that $$d_1$$ contains an invertible block of signed permutations. Let $$t=(x,y,z)$$ be a triangle, so $$I(x,z)=\{y\}$$. The faces $$(x,y)$$ and $$(y,z)$$ are links, because $$I(x,y)$$ and $$I(y,z)$$ are subsets of $$I(x,z)\setminus\{y\}=\varnothing$$. The face $$(x,z)$$ has one interior element. A second triangle containing $$(x,z)$$ would either have that same interior element, hence be $$t$$, or have $$(x,z)$$ as one of its two short faces, impossible because $$(x,z)$$ is not a link. So one-interior edges and triangles are in bijection. Order the columns of $$d_1$$ as (one-interior edges, links). Then $$d_1=[P\,|\,R]$$ with $$P$$ a signed permutation matrix, and $$L_2=PP^{T}+RR^{T}=\mathbb 1+RR^{T}\ge\mathbb 1$$. For the second statement, $$K^{2}=L_0\oplus L_1\oplus L_2$$ with $$L_1=d_0d_0^{T}+d_1^{T}d_1$$, and since $$d_1d_0=0$$ the nonzero spectrum of $$L_1$$ consists of that of $$d_0d_0^{T}$$ (equal to that of $$L_0$$) and that of $$d_1^{T}d_1$$ (equal to that of $$L_2$$). $$\square$$
</div>

The heart of the argument is the private-face bijection; the rest is linear algebra. With the counting form the numbers in $$K$$ are $$\pm1$$ independent of the density, so a mode of a physical Dirac operator on a region of linear size $$L_{\mathrm{reg}}$$ would appear at an eigenvalue of order $$\rho^{-1/2}/L_{\mathrm{reg}}\sim N^{-1/2}$$ in these units. Proposition 10 says that no such eigenvalue exists in the part of the spectrum that comes from $$L_2$$. The remaining sector is that of the vertex Laplacian $$L_0$$: it is the graph Laplacian of the one-skeleton of $$\Delta_1$$, and its smallest nonzero eigenvalue was found numerically to be about $$3$$, independent of $$N$$ (values $$3.29,3.09$$ at $$N=1000$$ and $$3.47,3.44$$ at $$N=2000$$; one sample at $$N=500$$ had a single low-degree vertex with $$0.85$$). That statement is an observation, not a theorem. With it, $$K$$ on $$\Delta_1$$ has no mode at the scale of the region in any sector.

<div class="proposition" markdown="1">
**Proposition 11** (degree-one mass). Let $$M_1=[d_0\,|\,d_1^{T}]$$ as a map from $$C^{0}\oplus C^{2}$$ to $$C^{1}$$, and $$r>0$$. The singular values of $$iK+r\Pi_1$$ are: $$0$$ with multiplicity $$b_0$$, $$r$$ with multiplicity $$\dim\mathrm{coker}M_1=b_1$$, and $$\tfrac12(\sqrt{r^{2}+4\mu}\mp r)$$ for each $$\mu$$ in the nonzero spectrum of $$L_0$$ together with the spectrum of $$L_2$$. The smallest nonzero singular value is $$\min\{r,\tfrac12(\sqrt{r^{2}+4}-r)\}$$.
</div>

<div class="proof" markdown="1">
*Proof.* Since $$d_1d_0=0$$, $$M_1^{T}M_1=L_0\oplus L_2$$. On each singular pair of $$M_1$$ with singular value $$s$$ the operator acts as $$\begin{pmatrix}0&is\\ is&r\end{pmatrix}$$ in a two-dimensional space, whose singular values have product $$s^{2}$$ and squares summing to $$2s^{2}+r^{2}$$, hence are $$\tfrac12(\sqrt{r^{2}+4s^{2}}\mp r)$$. The kernel of $$M_1$$ consists of the locally constant 0-cochains ($$d_1^{T}$$ is injective because $$L_2\ge1$$), and its cokernel is the space of harmonic 1-cochains, of dimension $$b_1$$, on which the operator is $$r$$. The bound uses Proposition 10. $$\square$$
</div>

The flat band of at least $$L-N+1$$ zero modes is lifted exactly to $$r$$, but nothing region-scale remains for it to leave behind. *Check.* For sprinklings with $$N=70$$ (230 links) and $$N=120$$ (428 links), the identity $$\chi=N-L$$ holds, $$\dim\ker K=162$$ and $$310$$ with $$\sum b_k=1+(L-N+1)$$, the Krein operator $$d-d^{T}$$ has the same kernel dimension, the smallest eigenvalue of $$L_2$$ is $$1$$ to $$10^{-14}$$, and the full singular-value list of $$iK+r\Pi_1$$ for $$r=0.5,1,2$$ agrees with Proposition 11 to $$3\times10^{-14}$$.

Four qualifiers belong to Propositions 8 to 10. (i) The combination is an anti-Hermitian Kähler–Dirac operator $$iK$$ plus a Hermitian term. Other phases are not covered. (ii) If $$W$$ is Hermitian and commutes with $$K$$, then on an eigenspace of $$K$$ with eigenvalue $$\lambda$$ the operator $$iK+W$$ is normal with singular values $$\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+w^{2}}\ge\lvert\lambda\rvert$$, so such a term cannot create light modes from modes of $$K$$ that are not light; a non-Hermitian $$\epsilon$$-even commuting $$W$$ is not covered. (iii) Degree masses are covered for a mass proportional to $$\Pi_1$$ (Proposition 11); other simplex-dependent masses are not examined. (iv) The statements concern the two-dimensional complex $$\Delta_1$$ with the counting form. For the Krein form $$K$$ is replaced by $$d-d^{T}$$, and Proposition 9 holds with the same proof because $$\lVert(d-d^{T})h\rVert^{2}=\lVert dh\rVert^{2}+\lVert d^{T}h\rVert^{2}$$. Proposition 10 does not apply to the four-dimensional complex $$\Delta_3$$, and "region scale" refers to the normalisation in which $$K$$ has entries $$\pm1$$.

## 5. The one-dimensional chain in 1+1 dimensions

In two dimensions the stabiliser of a causal diamond is trivial, and the rows for $$d=2$$ of Theorem 1 allow order-natural covariant kernels once a region frame is fixed. A kinetic term for a one-component chiral spinor still needs a null direction. Here that direction is a binary choice, and we examine the construction it allows.

Sprinkle $$N$$ points (with $$N$$ even) into the unit square in null coordinates and label them $$1,\dots,N$$ in increasing $$u$$. The commutator-type matrix of the order is $$C-C^{T}$$, where $$C_{xy}=1$$ if $$x\prec y$$. Since $$x\prec y$$ means that $$u$$ and $$v$$ both increase, $$(C-C^{T})_{xy}=\tfrac12[\mathrm{sgn}(u_y-u_x)+\mathrm{sgn}(v_y-v_x)]$$, that is $$C-C^{T}=-\tfrac12(S_u+S_v)$$, where $$(S_u)_{xy}=\mathrm{sgn}(u_x-u_y)$$ is the sign matrix of the linear order in $$u$$ and $$S_v$$ that of the order in $$v$$. The matrix splits into one piece for each null direction, each the sign matrix of a linear order. Choosing one of them, say $$S_u$$, which in the $$u$$-labelling has entries $$\mathrm{sgn}(r-s)$$, selects the chiral sector of fields depending on $$u$$. The choice is a binary datum: reflecting the diamond in space exchanges $$u$$ and $$v$$ and leaves the law of the sprinkling invariant, and no property of the order selects one side, in line with the absence of an intrinsic orientation [[Dowker–Sorkin]](#ref-ds).

Let $$V$$ be the antiperiodic shift, $$Ve_j=e_{j+1}$$ for $$j<N$$ and $$Ve_N=-e_1$$, so $$V^{N}=-\mathbb 1$$ and its eigenvalues are $$e^{i\theta_j}$$ with $$\theta_j=(2j-1)\pi/N$$. Then

$$
S_u=(\mathbb 1+V)(\mathbb 1-V)^{-1}, \tag{6}
$$

as one checks entry by entry (and numerically at $$N=40$$ to machine precision). The eigenvalues of the real antisymmetric matrix $$S_u$$ are $$i\cot(\theta_j/2)$$. The kinetic operator of a real (Majorana) one-component field with one chiral sector is $$K_u=S_u^{-1}$$, which discretises $$\partial_u/2N$$ (the matrix $$S_u$$ acts on a function sampled at the points as $$N$$ times the integral operator with kernel $$\mathrm{sgn}(u-u')$$, the inverse of $$\tfrac12\partial_u$$): its eigenvalues are $$-i\tan(\theta_j/2)$$, and $$D_u=-2iN\,S_u^{-1}$$ has eigenvalues $$-2N\tan(\theta_j/2)\approx-(2j-1)\pi\,$$ for $$\theta_j\ll1$$, the spectrum of $$-i\partial_u$$ on antiperiodic functions of $$u\in[0,1]$$.

<div class="proposition" markdown="1">
**Proposition 12** (one chiral sector, no low-mode doubler, maximal non-locality). For every $$N$$ and every sprinkling, $$\mathrm{spec}\,D_u=\{-2N\tan(\theta_j/2)\}$$. Because $$\theta\mapsto-2N\tan(\theta/2)$$ is strictly monotone on $$(-\pi,\pi)$$, for any $$\Lambda$$ the number of eigenvalues with $$\lvert\lambda\rvert<\Lambda$$ equals the number of antiperiodic modes $$\lvert(2n-1)\pi\rvert<\Lambda$$ up to $$O(\Lambda^{2}/N^{2})$$ relative corrections to the eigenvalues, so there is no second zero of the symbol. The inverse is $$S_u^{-1}=\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}(-1)^{k}V^{k}$$, so every off-diagonal entry of $$K_u$$ is $$\pm1$$: all pairs of elements are coupled with the same weight.
</div>

<div class="proof" markdown="1">
*Proof.* $$V$$ is diagonalised by the Fourier vectors with eigenvalues $$e^{i\theta_j}$$, and the Cayley map $$e^{i\theta}\mapsto(1+e^{i\theta})/(1-e^{i\theta})=i\cot(\theta/2)$$ gives the spectrum of $$S_u$$. Inverting gives $$-i\tan(\theta/2)$$, and the monotonicity statement is a property of $$\tan$$. For the inverse, $$S_u^{-1}=(\mathbb 1-V)(\mathbb 1+V)^{-1}$$ and $$(\mathbb 1+V)\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}(-V)^{k}=\mathbb 1-(-V)^{N}=2\cdot\mathbb 1$$ for even $$N$$ since $$V^{N}=-\mathbb 1$$. So $$S_u^{-1}=\tfrac12(\mathbb 1-V)\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}(-V)^{k}=\tfrac12\bigl[\mathbb 1+2\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}(-V)^{k}+(-V)^{N}\bigr]=\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}(-V)^{k}$$, which agrees with a direct numerical inversion at $$N=40$$. $$\square$$
</div>

The operator is therefore a non-local lattice derivative with a single zero of the symbol at the cost of couplings of equal magnitude between all elements. The question is the vacuum. A pure Gaussian state of the Majorana field is given by a real antisymmetric matrix $$\mathcal K$$ with $$\mathcal K^{2}=-\mathbb 1$$, through $$\langle\psi_r\psi_s\rangle=\tfrac12(\delta_{rs}+i\mathcal K_{rs})$$. The analogue of the Sorkin–Johnston prescription for scalar fields [[Surya]](#ref-surya) takes $$i\mathcal K$$ equal to the sign of the Hermitian matrix $$iS_u$$. We consider every state that is invariant under the shift $$V$$.

<div class="proposition" markdown="1">
**Proposition 13** (mirror theorem). Let $$\mathcal K$$ be real antisymmetric with $$\mathcal K^{2}=-\mathbb 1$$ and $$[\mathcal K,V]=0$$. Then $$\mathcal K$$ has a symbol $$f(\theta_j)\in\{+i,-i\}$$ which is an odd function of $$\theta$$. Consequently (i) $$f$$ changes sign between $$\theta=-\pi/N$$ and $$\theta=\pi/N$$ and between the two grid points adjacent to $$\theta=\pi$$; (ii) around the circle the number of upward jumps equals the number of downward jumps, so the net chirality $$c_L-c_R$$, defined as the signed number of jumps, vanishes for every such state. For the state with $$i\mathcal K=\mathrm{sgn}(iS_u)$$ the symbol is $$\mathrm{sgn}\sin\theta$$, with exactly these two jumps and no others, which is the sign of the symbol $$i\sin\theta$$ of the nearest-neighbour Majorana hopping chain, whose Fermi points are at $$\theta=0$$ and $$\theta=\pi$$.
</div>

<div class="proof" markdown="1">
*Proof.* A matrix commuting with $$V$$, which has distinct eigenvalues, is a polynomial in $$V$$ and is diagonalised by the same Fourier vectors; call its eigenvalue on the vector with phase $$\theta$$ the symbol $$f(\theta)$$. Reality of $$\mathcal K$$ gives $$f(-\theta)=\overline{f(\theta)}$$, antisymmetry gives $$f(-\theta)=-f(\theta)$$, so $$f$$ is purely imaginary and odd, and $$\mathcal K^{2}=-\mathbb 1$$ gives $$f\in\{\pm i\}$$. The grid $$\theta_j=(2j-1)\pi/N$$ is symmetric under $$\theta\to-\theta$$, with $$\pm\pi/N$$ adjacent at $$0$$ and $$\pm(N-1)\pi/N$$ adjacent at $$\pi$$ (using $$N$$ even), and oddness forces opposite signs at each pair. A cyclic sequence of $$\pm1$$ has as many upward as downward jumps. For the sign state, $$\mathrm{sgn}\cot(\theta/2)=\mathrm{sgn}\sin\theta$$ on $$(-\pi,\pi)\setminus\{0\}$$. $$\square$$
</div>

The first half is routine. The result is the lattice pairing of Fermi points in a form that applies to every invariant pure state, and it has one consequence for entanglement. For a conformal field theory the entanglement entropy of an interval of $$n$$ sites grows as $$\tfrac{c_L+c_R}{6}\ln n$$ [[Calabrese–Cardy]](#ref-cc), which is $$\tfrac16\ln n$$ for a non-chiral Majorana fermion ($$c_L=c_R=\tfrac12$$) and $$\tfrac1{12}\ln n$$ for a single Majorana–Weyl fermion. For the sign state at $$N=1000$$ and blocks of $$n=20,30,\dots,400$$ elements, a fit of the entropy of the Gaussian state to $$b\ln[(N/\pi)\sin(\pi n/N)]+\text{const}$$ gives $$b=0.16667$$, equal to $$1/6$$ to four digits. This is a numerical observation about one state, in agreement with the two Fermi points of Proposition 13. The entanglement entropy therefore sees both chiralities. A one-handed vacuum would need a state that is not shift invariant, or interactions, which Proposition 13 does not address.

## 6. The full order complex and the Möbius function

If the span restriction is dropped, the complex $$\Delta(P)$$ of all chains is not local, but the index of Theorem 5 has a closed form. Let $$\hat0$$ and $$\hat1$$ be the bottom and top of a causal diamond, $$P^{\circ}$$ the set of elements strictly between them, and $$\Delta(P^{\circ})$$ the order complex of $$P^{\circ}$$.

<div class="proposition" markdown="1">
**Proposition 14** (index of the open and closed interval). For every finite poset $$P^{\circ}$$, $$\chi(\Delta(P^{\circ}))=1+\mu(\hat0,\hat1)$$, so any operator as in Theorem 5 has $$\dim\ker K\ge\lvert1+\mu(\hat0,\hat1)\rvert$$ on $$\Delta(P^{\circ})$$. On the closed interval $$\Delta(P^{\circ}\cup\{\hat0,\hat1\})$$, a cone, $$\ker(d+d^{*})$$ is one-dimensional for the counting form.
</div>

<div class="proof" markdown="1">
*Proof.* Hall's theorem gives $$\mu(\hat0,\hat1)=\sum_{k\ge1}(-1)^{k}c_k$$, where $$c_k$$ is the number of chains $$\hat0\prec x_1\prec\dots\prec x_{k-1}\prec\hat1$$, that is the number of $$(k-1)$$-element chains of $$P^{\circ}$$ (for $$k=1$$, the empty chain). With $$C_j$$ the number of $$j$$-element chains and $$C_0=1$$, $$\mu=\sum_{j\ge0}(-1)^{j+1}C_j$$ while $$\chi(\Delta(P^{\circ}))=\sum_{j\ge1}(-1)^{j-1}C_j=1+\mu$$. A cone has the cohomology of a point. $$\square$$
</div>

In two dimensions the mean of this index is bounded.

<div class="proposition" markdown="1">
**Proposition 15** (mean index in two dimensions). Sprinkle the diamond $$[0,1]^{2}$$ in null coordinates with a Poisson process of mean $$\bar N$$ points and take $$\hat0=(0,0)$$, $$\hat1=(1,1)$$. Then $$\mathbb E[\chi(\Delta(P^{\circ}))]=1-J_0(2\sqrt{\bar N})$$, with $$J_0$$ the Bessel function. Since $$\lvert J_0\rvert\le1$$, the mean index lies between $$0$$ and $$2$$ for every $$\bar N$$.
</div>

<div class="proof" markdown="1">
*Proof.* By the Mecke formula the expected number of $$j$$-element chains is $$\frac{\bar N^{j}}{j!}$$ times the probability that $$j$$ independent uniform points form a chain. Sort them by $$u$$; they form a chain exactly when their $$v$$-ranks are increasing, with probability $$1/j!$$. So $$\mathbb E[C_j]=\bar N^{j}/(j!)^{2}$$, and Proposition 14 gives $$\mathbb E[\mu]=-\sum_j(-\bar N)^{j}/(j!)^{2}=-J_0(2\sqrt{\bar N})$$. The bound is $$\lvert J_0\rvert\le1$$. $$\square$$
</div>

The matrix $$(\mathbb 1+C)^{-1}$$, whose $$(\hat0,\hat1)$$ entry is $$\mu$$, is the sum over chains with weight $$-1$$ per step, the matrix geometric series that Johnston used to define retarded propagators [[Johnston 2008]](#ref-johnston), so $$-\mu/2$$ is the retarded propagator of that form with the step amplitude $$-1$$, and its mean is a two-dimensional massive propagator at a particular value of the mass parameter [[X–Dowker–Surya]](#ref-ads). The mean is bounded and the fluctuations are not. Monte Carlo sampling of this ensemble confirms the mean, giving $$\mathbb E\mu=-0.220\pm0.024$$ at $$\bar N=10$$ against $$-0.229$$, and $$0.180\pm0.063$$ at $$\bar N=30$$ against $$0.179$$, and gives $$\chi=1+\mu$$ by direct chain counting in all samples. The root-mean-square of $$\mu$$ is $$1.50$$, $$3.97$$ and $$27.9$$ at $$\bar N=10,30,100$$. These values are consistent with a growth $$\ln\mathrm{rms}\,\mu\propto\sqrt{\bar N}$$, which is an observation from three points and is not proved. On the closed interval the index is $$1$$ irrespective of $$\mu$$: the fluctuating index belongs to the open interval, which is the link of the edge $$\{\hat0,\hat1\}$$ in the closed complex.

## 7. What the results do not exclude

- Local frames. If each element carries its own frame, as when vierbeins are built from holonomies [[Sverdlov]](#ref-sverdlov), (H1) fails and Theorem 1 does not apply. The kernel then depends on frame data attached to the elements or pairs, as gauge data. Proposition 3 excludes an invariant random global frame; whether a gauge-fixed description with frames per element is consistent with the order is not decided here.
- A given global frame or region frame. Theorem 1 with $$H=SU(2)$$ allows scalar kernels, and a kinetic term then uses the frame explicitly; the transfer of such a frame into low-energy physics is the subject of [Where a preferred frame leaks into low-energy physics](/quantum-gravity/lorentz-violation-leakage/).
- Constructions covariant only on average, or only approximately, are not covered by (H3).
- Kähler–Dirac fields with terms that break the parity of the degree and are not of Hodge type; Wilson terms that do not commute with $$K$$ and are not degree-diagonal; degree masses other than $$\Pi_1$$.
- Inner products that are not degree-diagonal. A Lorentzian Whitney form is non-diagonal and indefinite; Theorem 5 does not apply to it and Propositions 9 and 10, which use positivity and the unit private-face entry, do not either.
- The complexes $$\Delta_{n_0}$$ with $$n_0\ge2$$ in two dimensions and the four-dimensional complex $$\Delta_3$$ for the Wilson-term statements. Proposition 9 is independent of the complex, but Proposition 10 is not.
- Regular lattices, for which the index test is passed (section 3), at the cost of the Lorentz symmetry.
- Other states and interactions on the 1+1 chain, such as a mirror fermion decoupled by interactions or a state not invariant under the shift.
- Link operators with signed weights, to which Proposition 4 does not apply.

## 8. Open points

It is not known whether the variance of the Möbius function of a random two-dimensional order grows like $$e^{c\sqrt{\bar N}}$$, as three sample values suggest. The sector of the vertex Laplacian of $$\Delta_1$$ has a gap of about $$3$$ in numerics, with no proof. Whether a Lorentzian inner product on the cochains of $$\Delta_1$$ yields region-scale modes, and whether the private-face weight stays bounded below for it, is not examined. The Kähler–Dirac construction on a Lorentzian manifold needs an operator that is incompatible with the Lorentz transformations [[Catterall–Hubisz]](#ref-ch), and the analogue of that condition on the order complex is not worked out. A four-dimensional version of the statement on Wilson terms, and the extension of Proposition 7 beyond two dimensions, are open.
